Two days ago, a friend of mine sent me a nice piece of music from a video game I
used to enjoy back in the PSX days,
[Ape Escape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ape_Escape), or _Saru! Get you!_
([サルゲッチュ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B5%E3%83%AB%E3%82%B2%E3%83%83%E3%83%81%E3%83%A5))
in Japanese. The game is about catching monkeys with a net. Even though the idea
seems basic, the game is really rich and entertaining, and the monkeys won't
miss a chance to play dirty tricks on you. Anyway, I brought this up because one
song particularly caught my attention.

The title of this song is "Wabi-Sabi Wall." As you all know, _wabi-sabi_ is the
green horseradish used in Japanese cuisine. It's very hot and… 違う！ That's
_wasabi_!

_Wabi-sabi_ is one of those Japanese aesthetics that are very hard to put into
words, especially in foreign languages, and by foreign I
mean 外国語 (_gaikokugo_). According to the English
[Wikipedia definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabi_sabi), _wabi-sabi_ (in
kanji: 侘寂) represents "a comprehensive Japanese world view or aesthetic
centred on the acceptance of transience." This confused me, since _mono no
aware_ means practically the same thing: "_Mono no aware_ (物の哀れ) is a
Japanese term used to describe the awareness of the transience of things, and a
gentle sadness at their passing." So I headed to the
[Japanese Wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%8F%E3%81%B3%E3%83%BB%E3%81%95%E3%81%B3)
to see how the word was defined in Japan.

The Japanese definition divides the term into two parts, corresponding to the
two kanji in the compound.

_Wabi_, the noun derived from the verb _wabu_, means "the superiority of
imperfection." That's a very approximate translation of _rippa na joutai ni
taisuru ototta joutai_, which literally gives "an inferior state compared with a
splendid one." I understand the example from Western culture, but I can't really
put it into words for fear of giving the wrong impression. In other words, we
could say "a humble, simple, or plain state or appearance." There isn't a
concrete concept that can explain it even in Japanese.

「侘（わび、侘びとも）とは、動詞「わぶ」の名詞形で、その意味は「立派な状態に対する劣った状態」となる。西洋文化にある状態で例えて言えば、パーティーが行われていないパーティー会場がこれに近い。転じては「粗末な様子」、あるいは「簡素な様子」を意味している。日本でも本来的は良い概念ではなかったが、これが積極的に評価され美意識の中にとりこまれていった。」

As for _sabi_, it is similarly the noun derived from the verb _sabu_, and it
means the deterioration of things as time passes. The English comparison is
"patina," a fine coating of oxide on the surface of a metal, usually green. In
my own words, the wear that appears in things, such as patina, repairs, and
rust, creates an atmosphere with a certain beauty to it. The appreciation of
this beauty can be described as _sabi_.

「寂（さび、寂びとも）は動詞「さぶ」の名詞形で、本来は時間の経過によって劣化した様子（経年変化）を意味している。…。 英語ではpatina（緑青）の美が類似のものとして挙げられ、緑青などが醸し出す雰囲気についてもpatinaと表現される。」

That said, combining the two kanji may give us something like "the beauty of
imperfection, incompleteness, and impermanence," or "the contemplation of the
imperfect, the transient, and what time has worn away."

Even though I tried my best to put my ideas into words, I'm still far from
capturing the concept. But that's nothing to worry about. Aesthetics are better
taken as a whole, understanding their essence without trying to limit them with
words. And that's what I appreciate about the traditional Japanese sense of
beauty (美意識, _biishiki_): subtlety!
